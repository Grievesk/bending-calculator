Why do we start with the formed inside radius?

Before calculating the flat pattern, we need to understand the inside radius produced by the bend. This gives us a sound starting point for deriving a K-factor that matches the bend geometry.

The Grievesk Bend calculation method

The sequence is straightforward: bending conditions → inside radius (measured or estimated) → K-factor → flat pattern. Each step depends on the one before it. If the radius is wrong, the K-factor and flat pattern can also be wrong.

1. A bend is not defined by the drawing alone

The drawing shows the required geometry: thickness, angle, dimensions and, when specified, the inside radius. On the press brake, the result also depends on the material, die opening, punch radius and bending method.

In air bending, the sheet rests on the two shoulders of the V-die and is pushed by the punch without reaching the bottom of the die. The resulting inside radius therefore does not necessarily equal the punch-tip radius. It depends mainly on the V opening and the material's behaviour, while tooling and working conditions also matter.

The first value to establish is the inside radius produced by the bend. If we can measure it on a representative test piece, we use that measurement. If it is not available, the calculator provides a model estimate: a starting point that must be checked on the actual part, not a real measurement.

How the current model handles it
For air bending, the model compares two values: one calculated from die opening V and the material's Rm range, and the punch's nominal radius Rp. In short: Ri = max(V × f(Rm), Rp). In the current model, f(Rm) can be 0.14, 0.16 or 0.20, depending on the selected tensile-strength range. This is an empirical relationship used by the calculator, not a rule for every material and tool. WingBend, coining and panel bending follow separate model paths.

In the current air-bending estimate, thickness does not directly change the inside radius. It enters the next step, where the calculator derives the K-factor from the inside-radius/thickness ratio and the bending method. Together with the inside angle, these values are then used to calculate the bend allowance and flat pattern.

2. Why the radius comes before K

The K-factor tells us where the neutral axis sits through the sheet thickness. It is the distance from the inside surface to the neutral axis divided by the sheet thickness. It is not the inside radius and it is not a fixed value for every material and bend.

During bending, the material on the inside is compressed while the outside is stretched. Between them is the neutral axis: the line treated as keeping the same length in the geometric model. Its position changes with the bend and working conditions. Using the same generic K-factor every time can therefore produce an inaccurate flat pattern.

Our method starts with the measured or estimated inside radius. The calculator uses it together with thickness and the bending method to derive a K-factor that matches the expected bend geometry. It does not assign a generic value without considering the bend being calculated.

3. From K to the flat pattern

Once we know the inside radius, thickness and K-factor, we can calculate the arc length along the neutral axis. This is called the Bend Allowance (BA). The standard geometric relationship is:

BA = θ × (Ri + K-factor × s)
θ is the bend angle in radians; Ri is the inside radius; s is sheet thickness. The K-factor locates the neutral axis within the thickness.

BA is the length of the curved portion measured along the neutral axis. Together with the outside dimensions and angle geometry, it is used to calculate the compensation needed to obtain the flat pattern.

4. What can be known — and what must be checked

  • Inputs: thickness, material/mechanical properties, die opening V, nominal punch radius, angle and bending method.
  • Central geometric value: formed inside radius, measured when available or estimated by the model.
  • Derived parameter: K-factor consistent with the neutral-axis position estimated by the model.
  • Output: bend allowance, bend deduction and flat length, according to the dimensions and geometric conventions used.

An estimate is not a measurement. Manufacturer tables, CAM data and test bends are valuable references, but they must be interpreted in context. For a specific production setup, the most reliable value is obtained from a representative test piece, measured after bending and checked against the actual dimensions.

The current model does not claim to describe every material, tool and bending method with one universal law. Empirical relationships apply within the range for which they were collected and checked; outside that range, the result should be treated as indicative, not as a production guarantee.

5. Why this approach helps on the shop floor

Changing the V opening changes the bending conditions and can change the inside radius obtained. If the radius changes, the neutral-axis position changes too, and so does the length assigned to the curved portion. Even when the drawing and outside dimensions remain the same, the flat pattern may need a correction.

Keeping these steps separate makes the result easier to understand: not just “which K-factor should I use?”, but which radius do we expect from this setup, and how confident are we in that estimate?

First understand the radius produced by the bend. Then derive the K-factor. Finally calculate the flat pattern.
