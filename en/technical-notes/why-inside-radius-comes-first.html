Why do we start with the formed inside radius?

Before calculating how much material is needed in the flat pattern, we need to understand the geometry the sheet takes on during bending. The K-factor should not be an arbitrary number: it must be consistent with that geometry.

The Grievesk Bend calculation method

The sequence is deliberate: bending conditions → formed (or estimated) inside radius → K-factor → flat pattern. It keeps the process geometry separate from a coefficient selected by convention.

1. A bend is not defined by the drawing alone

The engineering drawing describes the nominal geometry: thickness, angle, dimensions and, when specified, the inside radius. On the press brake, however, the sheet is deformed by a combination of conditions: material and its mechanical properties, actual thickness, die opening and geometry, punch radius, bending method and process settings.

In air bending, the sheet rests on the two shoulders of the V-die and is pushed by the punch without being fully pressed against the bottom of the die. The resulting inside radius therefore does not automatically equal the nominal punch-tip radius. Die opening and material behaviour play an important role, while the punch and the other process conditions remain part of the picture.

The first value to determine is therefore the inside radius formed by the operation. If it has been measured on a representative test piece, that measurement is the best starting point. If it is not available, the calculator can provide a model estimate, with its assumptions stated and a reminder that it must be checked on the actual part.

How the current model handles it
For air bending, Grievesk Bend estimates Ri as the greater of a value derived from die opening V and the material's Rm band, and the punch's nominal radius Rp: Ri = max(V × f(Rm), Rp). In the current model f(Rm) is 0.14, 0.16 or 0.20 depending on the selected tensile-strength band. This is a declared empirical relationship, not a universal law. WingBend and coining use the punch radius as a method-specific assumption; panel bending follows a separate estimate.

In the current air-bending branch, thickness does not directly change the Ri estimate: it enters the next step, where K is estimated from the Ri/thickness ratio and the bending method. The inside angle is then converted into the bend angle used for bend allowance and deduction. These steps matter because the input variables do not all act in the same way.

2. Why the radius comes before K

The K-factor describes the position of the neutral axis through the sheet thickness. It is the distance from the inside surface to the neutral axis divided by sheet thickness. It is not the radius, and it is not a universal material constant.

During bending, the inner side is compressed and the outer side is in tension. Between them lies the neutral axis, the fibre whose length is treated as unchanged in the geometric model. Its position changes with bend severity and process conditions, so a generic K-factor may not describe a particular combination of material, thickness, tooling and method.

In our approach, the estimated or measured inside radius provides the geometric reference from which a K-factor consistent with the bend is derived. K is related to thickness and the neutral-axis position; it is not copied from a generic table or inferred from the punch-tip radius alone.

3. From K to the flat pattern

Once the inside radius, thickness and K-factor are established, we can calculate the arc length along the neutral axis, known as the Bend Allowance (BA). The standard geometric relationship is:

BA = θ × (Ri + K × s)
θ is the bend angle in radians; Ri is the inside radius; s is sheet thickness; K locates the neutral axis within the thickness.

BA is the length of the curved portion measured along the neutral axis. Together with the outside dimensions and angle geometry, it is used to calculate the compensation needed to obtain the flat pattern.

4. What can be known — and what must be checked

  • Inputs: thickness, material/mechanical properties, die opening V, nominal punch radius, angle and bending method.
  • Central geometric value: formed inside radius, measured when available or estimated by the model.
  • Derived parameter: K-factor consistent with the neutral-axis position estimated by the model.
  • Output: bend allowance, bend deduction and flat length, according to the dimensions and geometric conventions used.

An estimate is not a measurement. Manufacturer tables, CAM data and test bends are valuable references, but they must be interpreted in context. For a specific production setup, the most reliable value is obtained from a representative test piece, measured after bending and checked against the actual dimensions.

The current model does not claim to describe every material, tool and bending method with one universal law. Empirical relationships apply within the range for which they were collected and checked; outside that range, the result should be treated as indicative, not as a production guarantee.

5. Why this approach helps on the shop floor

Changing the V opening changes the forming conditions and can change the inside radius obtained. If the radius changes, the neutral-axis position changes too, and so does the length assigned to the curved portion. Even when the drawing and outside dimensions remain the same, the flat pattern may need a correction.

Keeping these steps separate makes the result easier to understand: not just “which K should I use?”, but which radius do we expect from this setup, and how confident are we in that estimate?

First the bend that is actually formed. Then the K-factor that describes it. Finally, the flat pattern that follows.
